A decision letter in six pieces

This browser worksheet assembles your own words into a letter. Use initials or fictional details if you prefer. Nothing is saved here; copy your letter before leaving. It does not send a letter, set a reminder or transfer anything into the app.

The record

Loading the example replaces these fields. This is a writing aid, not advice about which option to choose.

Your letter · updates as you write

Dear future me, Before I know how this turns out, I want to leave a clear record. [The change I am choosing] [What I know today] [What I am assuming or hoping] [What I considered instead] [Why this option, with its trade-off] [What I want to ask later] From the version of me who is choosing today.

If copying is unavailable, select the letter text and copy it manually. Blank sections remain visible as prompts; they are not filled in for you.

Separate the known from the hoped-for.

“The class meets on Saturday” is a checkable fact. “I will make friends there” is a hope. Both can belong in the letter, but give them different labels. If a detail is second-hand, say where it came from and what remains unconfirmed.

Name the option you left behind.

Write one realistic alternative, including doing nothing for now. Record why you passed on it. This makes the trade-off visible without pretending that every option could have been tested.

Ask questions that allow surprise.

“Was I right?” invites a single verdict. “Which assumption held up?” and “What would I adjust?” give future you more to work with. The review can describe an outcome without grading your past self.

When you read it again

Keep the original letter intact. Add a dated reply underneath or in a separate note. Start with what happened, then compare it with what you expected. Identify information that only became available later. A pleasant outcome does not mean every assumption was sound; an awkward outcome does not erase the reasons you had at the time.

For the pottery example, a useful reply might be: “I liked the making but found the early start difficult. My assumption about enjoying the activity held up. My assumption about Saturday mornings did not. Next time I would try an afternoon session.”

Read a fictional example
Original line: “Will I ever find my way around this neighborhood?” Reply: “I walked home from the library without looking at the map. I still take the long way when the bakery is open.” Next time: “Which corner do you know by its smell?”
Do I have to make a prediction?
No. You can write “I do not know yet.” The point is to preserve your current understanding, including its gaps. Do not invent a probability just to make the letter look precise.
When should I reopen the letter?
Choose a moment when there may be something new to observe, such as after the trial period you already planned. This web page does not schedule delivery. If you use an app, choose from the timing options the app actually provides.
Is this for major medical, legal or financial decisions?
It can record questions in your own words, but it does not assess risk or replace qualified advice. The worked example is deliberately an ordinary activity choice.
