Exactly what this tool checks

It looks only for eight English words or phrases, ignoring capitalization: nice, amazing, great, special, always, everything, a lot, and things. It uses whole-word matches. It does not understand your story, judge tone or detect writing quality. You decide whether a prompt helps.

Read the complete facilitator guide

  1. 0-2 minutes | Set the boundary

    Say: "This is a writing practice, not a sharing test. Choose an ordinary, comfortable moment. You may invent a scene, skip a prompt, leave or keep every word private. Nobody collects the letters. Reading aloud is optional."

  2. 2-5 minutes | Notice the model

    Read the fictional letter on page 2. Ask: Which object can you picture? Which sentence tells us what the writer thinks? Where does the letter leave a question unanswered? Discuss the model, not anybody's private experience.

  3. 5-10 minutes | Draft quietly

    Use page 3. Choose a room, journey or small task. Write one object, one action and one thought. Add a question for a later you. Fragments count; there is no minimum length. Offer the fictional-scene option again.

  4. 10-15 minutes | Revise one line

    Underline one broad phrase such as "a great day." Try a replacement that a camera or microphone could notice. Keep the feeling if it matters. Never invent a quotation or memory to satisfy a prompt.

  5. 15-18 minutes | Optional response

    Invite writers to share one chosen sentence, pass, or talk only about the model. Listeners answer: "The detail I could picture was..." Do not interpret the writer, ask for personal history or prescribe a better ending.

  6. 18-20 minutes | Keep or discard

    Each person decides what to keep. Take the paper home, copy it into an existing notebook, or discard it. The free browser workshop is optional. An app install is never needed to complete the session.

Read the complete fictional model letter

Dear future me,

I am writing at the kitchen table on a rainy Thursday. My blue mug rocks unless I slide a folded napkin under it. There is a library book beside the kettle that I have renewed twice.

This morning I took the long way home from the shop. Someone had tied a red ribbon to a bicycle basket. I stopped to look at it instead of reaching for my phone. I would probably forget that minute if I did not write it here.

I keep thinking I need a dramatic story before I can begin a letter. Today I only have a wobbly mug, a ribbon and a book I still want to finish. That feels like enough to record.

When you read this, what is on your table? If the book is finished, was there a sentence you wanted to keep? You do not need to have changed anything. I am just curious about the ordinary day you are having.

From the me at this table

Discuss the writing, not the writer

Which detail do you remember after looking away? What remains unknown? Which line would you keep without making it longer?

Broad line: “It was a special morning.” Specific line: “We ate toast on the kitchen floor while the table paint dried.” The revision names an action and setting. It does not prove the morning felt special; the writer can add that feeling in their own words.

Read the participant prompts
  1. Place one small thing in the scene. An object, sound or action you can describe without guessing.
  2. Write the thought beside it. What did you notice, wonder or want to remember?
  3. Ask a later you one question. It can stay unanswered. No prediction or promise is required.
  4. Revise one sentence. Replace a broad phrase with one detail. Keep wording that sounds like you.

Choose a real ordinary scene, an invented scene, or skip anything. Nobody needs to read your work. Keep it, copy into a notebook, revise later or discard.

USE IT IN REAL LIFE

A short revision you can do yourself.

  1. Pick one broad sentence. Leave the rest of the letter alone for now.
  2. Ask what a camera or microphone could catch. Name a place, an action, an object or a remembered phrase. If you do not remember the exact words, describe the moment without inventing a quotation.
  3. Add what it meant to you. A detail can sit beside a feeling. You do not have to replace warmth with a description of furniture.
  4. Read it in your own voice. Short, ordinary words are enough. A private letter does not need to sound published.

One more fictional before and after

Before: “You always helped me.”

After: “When my bicycle chain slipped on the way to school, you walked beside me the rest of the way.”

“Always” is not banned. The example simply gives a future reader a moment to hold onto. Use the same approach in a letter to future you, a thank-you note or a private journal entry.
